Forza Horizon 6 Is the Open-World Racing Game We’ve Been Waiting For

Created2026-05-15|Updated2026-05-20|World Affairs

Forza Horizon 6 Is the Open-World Racing Game We’ve Been Waiting For

As a Chinese gaming blogger who’s been obsessed with open-world racing games since my teens—growing up playing Need for Speed Underground and Initial D arcade machines in my hometown of Guangzhou—I can say with absolute certainty: For years, fans like me have dreamed about one thing, and one thing only.

A Forza Horizon game set in Japan.

For Chinese players, Japan’s car culture isn’t just a trend—it’s a lifelong obsession. We grew up watching Initial D on DVD, debating the merits of AE86 vs. RX-7 with friends after school, and drooling over JDM legends in car magazines that were hard to find in local bookstores. Neon-lit Tokyo highways, touge mountain drifting, rain-soaked streets glowing under vending machine lights, endless coastal roads beneath Mount Fuji—these aren’t just visuals to us; they’re the stuff of childhood dreams, the kind of scenes we’ve imagined racing through for decades.

Now, that dream has finally become reality.

With the release of Forza Horizon 6, Playground Games has delivered not just another racing game, but arguably the most ambitious open-world driving experience ever created. As someone who has spent hundreds of hours in every Forza Horizon title—from FH3’s Australian outback to FH5’s Mexican deserts—I went into FH6 with high expectations, but nothing prepared me for how deeply it would resonate with my love for JDM culture and open-world freedom.

And after spending dozens of hours exploring its roads—late at night, on weekends, even sneaking in a few races during my lunch break—it’s hard not to feel like this is the moment the Horizon series truly reached another level. For Chinese players like me, it’s more than a game; it’s a love letter to everything we’ve ever admired about Japanese car culture.

Where to Learn More

As a Chinese gaming blogger, I always recommend my followers check official sources for the latest updates—nothing beats firsthand information. Players can visit the official Forza website for updates, expansion details, and future seasonal content, and the Steam page is perfect for PC players in China who prefer using the platform to manage their games (though many of us also use Xbox Game Pass for seamless access).

  • Official Forza Horizon Website

  • Steam Franchise Page

Japan Finally Becomes the Perfect Horizon Playground

The biggest headline is obvious: FH6 takes the franchise to Japan for the very first time. But for Chinese players, this isn’t just a “new setting”—it’s the fulfillment of a decades-long wish. We’ve spent years modding older Horizon games to add Japanese cars, customizing liveries to match our favorite Initial D characters, and even creating fan-made routes that mimic touge mountain roads. Now, we don’t have to mod anymore; Playground Games has built the Japan we’ve always wanted, and it’s even better than we imagined.

But this isn’t a small interpretation of Japan. It’s massive. The map is reportedly around 25% larger than the previous game, blending together regions that feel distinct yet cohesive—exactly what we look for in an open-world racing game. As a Chinese player who loves both urban racing and scenic exploration, I was blown away by how well FH6 balances the chaos of city streets with the serenity of countryside roads.

The map blends together:

  • dense Tokyo city streets, where I’ve spent hours weaving through traffic, pretending I’m in a Wangan Midnight-style highway battle—something my friends and I used to reenact in FH4 with modded cars;

  • mountain touge roads, narrow and winding, just like the ones we’ve seen in Initial D—perfect for drifting, which is a huge part of Chinese racing game culture;

  • coastal highways, where I often cruise at sunset, listening to J-pop playlists (yes, the in-game radio has great J-pop and city pop tracks, which are wildly popular among Chinese JDM fans);

  • countryside villages, quiet and charming, a nice break from the chaos of Tokyo—ideal for “vibe driving,” which I’ll talk about later;

  • volcanic regions, with dramatic landscapes that make for stunning photos (I’ve already shared dozens on my Weibo and Bilibili accounts, and my followers are obsessed);

  • snowy mountain passes near Mount Fuji, where winter driving becomes a whole new challenge—something we rarely get to experience in Chinese racing games, which mostly focus on warm climates;

  • hidden temples and forests, perfect for exploring off the beaten path—my favorite is a small temple nestled in the mountains, where I often park my AE86 and just take in the view.

The result feels less like a racing map and more like a living road-trip fantasy. One moment you&#39;re blasting through Tokyo at midnight surrounded by glowing billboards and traffic reflections—reminding me of the neon-lit streets of Guangzhou’s Tianhe District, but with a distinctly Japanese flair. The next, you&#39;re drifting down narrow mountain roads while cherry blossoms scatter across the windshield—something I’ve only seen in anime and movies, but now get to experience firsthand in a game.

The contrast is what makes FH6 special. Japan gives the game personality in a way previous Horizon settings never fully achieved. Australia was fun, Mexico was vibrant, but Japan feels personal—especially for Chinese players who grew up immersed in Japanese car and pop culture. It’s not just a place to race; it’s a place that feels familiar, even if we’ve never been there in real life.

Tokyo at Night Is Unreal

The Tokyo-inspired urban area might be the most visually impressive city Playground Games has ever built. As a Chinese player who loves city racing—there’s nothing like the thrill of weaving through traffic in a busy metropolis—it’s everything I’ve ever wanted in a Horizon game. Shibuya-style intersections pulse with traffic and neon lights, just like the scenes we’ve seen in Tokyo Xtreme Racer and Need for Speed Underground, two games that were hugely popular in China when I was a kid.

Elevated expressways twist through skyscrapers, creating a maze of roads that feel both chaotic and exciting. Tiny side streets hide underground garages, tuning shops, and secret race meetups—exactly the kind of places we imagine when we think of Japanese street racing culture. I spent an entire evening exploring these side streets, finding hidden tuning shops that let me customize my car with JDM parts, something that felt like a nod to the modding community in China (we’re obsessed with customizing our in-game cars to look like the ones in our favorite anime).

At night, the city becomes almost hypnotic. Rain reflects thousands of lights onto the pavement, giving the game a cinematic atmosphere that feels heavily inspired by Japanese street racing culture and classic midnight highway driving scenes. As someone who often plays racing games late at night (after finishing work on my blog), this vibe hits differently—it’s calming yet thrilling, the perfect way to unwind after a long day.

If you grew up loving:

  • Initial D (the anime that sparked my love for JDM cars and touge racing—my friends and I still quote Takumi and Bunta to this day);

  • Wangan Midnight (the manga and game that made highway racing feel cool—we used to pretend we were driving the Blackbird when we played FH3);

  • Tokyo Xtreme Racer (a cult classic in China, even though it was hard to find legitimate copies);

  • Need for Speed Underground (the game that introduced many Chinese players to street racing and car customization);

then FH6 feels like a modern evolution of those fantasies. It’s not just a nostalgia trip, though—it’s a fresh take on everything we loved about those games, with better graphics, smoother physics, and a world that feels alive. For Chinese players, this is more than a game; it’s a chance to relive our childhoods while experiencing something new.

The Mountain Roads Steal the Show

As beautiful as Tokyo is, the real magic begins in the mountains. For Chinese JDM fans, touge culture is sacred—we’ve spent years watching Initial D, debating the best drift techniques, and even practicing on virtual touge roads in older games. FH6 fully embraces Japan’s legendary touge culture, and it does it better than any game I’ve ever played.

Narrow downhill roads snake through forests and cliffs, creating some of the best drifting routes ever seen in an open-world racing game. The roads feel authentic—they’re not too wide, not too smooth, and they have the kind of curves that make drifting both challenging and rewarding. I’ve spent hours perfecting my drift on these roads, using the same techniques I learned from Initial D (yes, I still try to “feather the throttle” like Takumi).

This is where cars like the:

  • AE86 (the iconic Trueno, a favorite among Chinese JDM fans—we call it the “Little Red Car” and often customize it to look like Takumi’s);

  • RX-7 (especially the FD, which is a status symbol in Chinese racing game communities—everyone wants to mod it with a big turbo and a wide body);

  • Silvia S15 (a drift legend that’s hard to find in real life, but FH6 gives us the chance to own and customize it to our heart’s content);

  • Skyline GT-R (the “Godzilla” of JDM cars—Chinese fans are obsessed with the R34, and FH6’s version is so detailed, you can even see the tiny logos on the engine);

  • Honda NSX (a supercar that’s both fast and beautiful—perfect for both racing and cruising, which is why it’s one of my go-to cars);

feel truly alive. The improved physics system makes drifting smoother and more believable than before. Cars transition naturally between grip and slide, while road surfaces react differently depending on weather and season—something that adds a level of realism I’ve never seen in a Horizon game.

And yes — weather finally matters. As a Chinese player who’s used to games where weather is just a visual effect, this was a game-changer. Driving through mountain fog during heavy rain at night is genuinely tense—you have to slow down, pay attention to the road, and adjust your drifting technique. It’s not just about going fast; it’s about mastering the conditions, which makes every race feel more rewarding. I’ve had moments where I almost crashed into a cliff because of the fog, but that’s part of the fun—it feels real, like you’re actually driving a powerful car on a dangerous mountain road.

Dynamic Seasons Completely Change the World

FH6’s seasonal system is more immersive than anything the series has done before. For Chinese players, who experience four distinct seasons in most parts of the country (I grew up in Guangzhou, where winters are mild but summers are hot and rainy), this feels incredibly relatable. Instead of simply changing visuals, seasons transform the driving experience itself—something that makes the world feel alive and ever-changing.

I’ve spent time exploring the map in every season, and each one offers a unique experience. As a blogger, I love taking photos of the same locations in different seasons—my followers go crazy for the cherry blossom photos in spring and the snow-covered Mount Fuji in winter.

Spring

Cherry blossoms explode across roadsides and parks. This is my favorite season in FH6—there’s something magical about driving through a tunnel of cherry blossoms, with petals sticking to my car’s windshield. It reminds me of the cherry blossom trees in Wuhan’s East Lake, a popular spot for Chinese tourists. The roads are dry and perfect for cruising, and the scenery is so beautiful, I often stop mid-drive to take photos. It’s the perfect season for “vibe driving,” which I’ll talk about later.

Summer

Bright sunlight and dry pavement create ideal racing conditions. Summer in FH6 feels like summer in China—hot, bright, and full of energy. The coastal roads are perfect for high-speed runs, and the countryside villages are bustling with activity. I often drive my NSX along the coast during summer, with the top down (if the car has one) and the radio turned up. It’s a great season for competitive races, as the dry pavement gives you maximum grip.

Autumn

Orange forests and falling leaves make every mountain road look like a wallpaper. Autumn in FH6 is stunning— the mountains are covered in red and orange leaves, and the light is soft and warm. It’s the perfect season for drifting, as the slightly cooler weather makes the roads less slippery. I often take my AE86 up to the mountains in autumn, drifting through the leaves and enjoying the scenery. It’s a calm, peaceful season that feels like a break from the chaos of Tokyo.

Winter

Snow completely changes handling, especially in high-altitude regions near Mount Fuji. For Chinese players who live in southern China (like me), snow is a rare treat—so driving in FH6’s winter season feels like a novelty. The roads are slippery, and you have to adjust your driving style to avoid sliding. Some roads become dangerous, while others become perfect for rally driving. I’ve spent hours rallying through snow-covered mountains in my modified Subaru WRX, and it’s one of the most fun experiences in the game. It’s a reminder that FH6 isn’t just about speed—it’s about adapting to the world around you.

The world constantly feels fresh. Even after dozens of hours, I still find new things to explore in each season. One week I’m cruising through cherry blossoms in spring, the next I’m rallying through snow in winter. It’s a level of variety that keeps the game from feeling repetitive, which is a huge plus for Chinese players who love sinking hundreds of hours into open-world games.

550+ Cars and the Best JDM Collection Yet

Car culture has always been the heart of Horizon, and FH6 goes all in. For Chinese players, this is make-or-break—we’re obsessed with car variety, especially JDM cars. The game launches with over 550 vehicles, including one of the strongest Japanese car lineups ever seen in a racing game. JDM fans are eating well here, and as someone who’s spent years collecting JDM cars in previous Horizon games, I’m in heaven.

Classic legends return alongside modern supercars and rare collector vehicles. Whether you want a classic AE86 or a modern Nissan GT-R Nismo, FH6 has you covered. What I love most is that Playground Games didn’t just include popular JDM cars—they included rare gems that even diehard fans might not have heard of. I recently found a 1990s Mitsubishi Lancer Evolution III in the used-car market, and I’ve been customizing it ever since. It’s the kind of detail that shows Playground Games truly understands JDM culture.

Whether you want:

  • a perfectly tuned drift machine (like my modified AE86, which I’ve set up with a turbo, lowered suspension, and a custom livery inspired by Initial D);

  • a ridiculous off-road monster (great for exploring the volcanic regions and snow-covered mountains);

  • a hypercar for highway runs (the Bugatti Chiron is perfect for blasting through Tokyo’s expressways);

  • or a fully customized street build (I’ve seen Chinese players online create some amazing builds, from wide-body RX-7s to lowrider Hondas);

FH6 gives you endless freedom. Customization is also deeper than before, which is a huge win for Chinese players—we love customizing our cars to stand out. I spend hours in the customization menu, tweaking every detail of my cars to make them unique. It’s not just about performance; it’s about making the car feel like yours.

Players can modify:

  • suspension (lowering it for a stance that’s popular in Chinese JDM communities);

  • gearing (to make the car faster on highways or better for drifting);

  • braking (essential for touge racing, where you need to stop quickly around tight corners);

  • turbo setups (to add more power and that iconic JDM “spool” sound);

  • aero parts (to improve downforce and make the car look more aggressive);

  • body kits (wide-body kits are a must for any JDM build, in my opinion);

  • wheel fitment (I spend more time on this than I’d like to admit—getting the perfect offset is key);

  • liveries (I’ve created custom liveries inspired by Chinese street art and my favorite anime, which I share with my followers on Bilibili).

The used-car market is another smart addition. Hunting for hidden gems and pre-tuned builds adds personality to progression instead of simply buying everything instantly. For Chinese players, this feels like a nod to our love of “treasure hunting”—we love searching for rare cars, just like we used to search for rare anime DVDs or car magazines as kids. I recently found a pre-tuned Silvia S15 in the used-car market, and it’s become one of my favorite cars to drive. It’s a small detail, but it makes the game feel more rewarding.

FH6 Understands That Driving Can Be Relaxing

One of the smartest design choices in FH6 is that it no longer treats racing as the only goal. As a Chinese gaming blogger, I often talk about “relaxing gaming” on my platform—many of my followers are busy professionals who play games to unwind, not to compete. Playground Games clearly understands why many people love open-world driving games in the first place: sometimes players just want to drive.

The game introduces a stronger focus on what developers call“vibe driving.” And honestly, that description fits perfectly. For Chinese players, this is a game-changer—we don’t always want to race; sometimes we just want to cruise, listen to music, and take in the scenery. FH6 lets us do that, and it does it better than any other racing game I’ve played.

You can spend hours:

  • cruising along coastal roads at sunset, listening to J-pop or city pop (the in-game radio has a great selection, and I often add my own playlists via the game’s custom radio feature);

  • driving through quiet villages, waving at NPCs and taking photos of the traditional Japanese architecture (which reminds me of the ancient towns in China’s Jiangsu province);

  • listening to music during rainstorms, watching the raindrops hit the windshield and the neon lights reflect on the wet pavement—it’s incredibly calming;

  • taking photos near Mount Fuji, which is one of the most photogenic spots in the game. I’ve taken hundreds of photos of my cars in front of Mount Fuji, and my followers love them—they often ask me for tips on how to get the perfect shot;

  • exploring hidden roads with friends. Many Chinese players play FH6 with friends online, and we often spend hours cruising together, chatting about cars and life. It’s a great way to connect with friends, especially if we’re in different cities.

without touching a single competitive race. FH6 feels comfortable letting players exist inside its world instead of constantly pushing objectives. That balance between intensity and relaxation is part of what makes the experience so addictive. As someone who often plays games to unwind after a long day of writing blog posts, this is exactly what I need. I can hop into FH6, cruise for an hour, and feel completely refreshed.

Online Features Feel More Alive Than Ever

The multiplayer side of FH6 also feels far more natural. For Chinese players, online gaming is all about community—we love playing with friends, joining clans, and participating in group activities. FH6’s online features tap into that perfectly, creating a community that feels alive and welcoming.

Car meetups inspired by Japanese parking-area culture create social spaces where players gather organically before races. Instead of disconnected menus, the world itself becomes the online lobby. This is a huge improvement over previous Horizon games, where online play felt disconnected from the open world. Now, you can drive around, find a group of players, and join a race or drift session without ever leaving the game world.

Street races start dynamically. Random highway challenges appear naturally. Drift groups form in mountain regions late at night. For the first time in the series, Horizon genuinely feels like a real car community rather than just a checklist of events. As a Chinese player, this feels familiar—we love gathering with friends to talk about cars, and FH6’s online features let us do that in a virtual setting.

I often join drift groups in the mountains late at night, where players from all over the world (including other Chinese players) gather to show off their drift skills. We take turns drifting down the touge roads, giving each other tips and cheering each other on. It’s a fun, friendly community that feels like a virtual version of the car meetups we see in Japanese movies. It’s not just about winning; it’s about sharing a love of cars.

The Visuals Are Ridiculous

On high-end PC hardware, FH6 is stunning. As a Chinese gaming blogger who reviews games on both PC and console, I was blown away by how good FH6 looks. Between the detailed car models, the realistic weather effects, and the beautiful landscapes, it’s one of the best-looking games I’ve ever played. Many of my followers have asked me if the game is “worth upgrading their PC for,” and my answer is always yes—especially if you love racing games.

Between:

  • ray tracing, which makes reflections look incredibly realistic—you can see every detail of your car in the puddles on the road;

  • dense weather effects, like rain that soaks your car and fog that makes distant objects look hazy;

  • improved lighting, which makes the world feel more vibrant and alive—sunlight filters through trees, neon lights glow in the dark, and streetlights cast warm shadows;

  • 4K support, which makes every detail pop—you can see the tiny logos on the car’s engine, the texture of the road, and the petals on the cherry blossoms;

  • DLSS technologies, which keep the game running smoothly even at high settings—no more frame drops during intense races;

  • detailed reflections, which make the city feel like a mirror—neon lights reflect on wet pavement, and buildings reflect in the river;

the game frequently looks photorealistic. I’ve had moments where I stopped mid-drive, thinking I was looking at a photo instead of a game. The attention to detail is incredible—from the way the cherry blossoms fall to the way the rain drips off the car’s windshield, every little detail makes the world feel real.

What’s more impressive is how stable everything feels even at high speeds. Despite the giant map and visual complexity, performance remains surprisingly smooth. As a Chinese player who’s used to AAA games that are buggy or poorly optimized, this was a nice surprise. FH6 feels polished in a way very few modern AAA releases manage to achieve. I’ve played for dozens of hours and haven’t encountered a single bug or frame drop, which is a testament to Playground Games’ attention to detail.

Is Forza Horizon 6 Worth It?

Absolutely. As a Chinese gaming blogger who’s reviewed dozens of racing games, I can say without hesitation that FH6 is worth every penny. It’s not just a great racing game; it’s a great game, period. For Chinese players, it’s even more special—it’s a love letter to JDM culture, a chance to experience the Japan we’ve always dreamed of, and a relaxing escape from the chaos of daily life.

FH6 doesn’t reinvent the Horizon formula completely, but it refines almost every part of it. It understands exactly what players love about open-world racing:

  • freedom to explore, without being forced to race;

  • atmosphere that makes you feel like you’re really in Japan;

  • car culture that celebrates JDM legends and modern supercars;

  • exploration of a massive, diverse world;

  • speed that’s thrilling but not overwhelming;

  • personalization that lets you make your cars unique.

Most importantly, it captures emotion. There’s a unique feeling that happens during late-night drives in FH6 — neon lights reflecting on wet asphalt, engine noise echoing through mountain tunnels, distant city lights glowing below the cliffs — where the game stops feeling like a racing simulator and starts feeling like a place you want to stay in. It’s the same feeling I get when I’m driving through Guangzhou’s neon-lit streets at night, or when I’m watching Initial D with my friends. It’s a feeling of joy, nostalgia, and freedom.

That’s what makes Forza Horizon 6 special. It isn’t just about racing faster. It’s about falling in love with driving again. For Chinese players who grew up loving JDM culture and open-world racing games, this is the game we’ve been waiting for.

Final Thoughts

Forza Horizon 6 may end up being remembered as the defining open-world racing game of this generation. As a Chinese gaming blogger, I’ve never been more excited about a racing game. It’s not just that it’s set in Japan (though that’s a huge plus); it’s that it understands what players love about open-world racing, and it delivers it in a way that feels personal and authentic.

By combining:

  • Japanese car culture (something that’s near and dear to Chinese JDM fans);

  • massive open-world freedom (perfect for exploring and “vibe driving”);

  • deep customization (so we can make our cars feel like ours);

  • dynamic weather (which adds realism and variety);

  • social online systems (that let us connect with friends and other car lovers);

  • and breathtaking visuals (that make the world feel real);

Playground Games has created something that feels bigger than a normal racing title. It’s a world to explore, a community to join, and a love letter to car culture. Whether you&#39;re a hardcore sim fan, a casual player, or someone who simply loves cars, FH6 offers one of the most immersive driving experiences available today.

And honestly?

After all these years, Horizon finally found its perfect setting. For Chinese players like me, it’s more than a game—it’s a dream come true. I’ve already spent dozens of hours in FH6, and I know I’ll spend hundreds more. It’s the kind of game that stays with you, long after you’ve turned off your PC or console. It’s the kind of game that makes you fall in love with driving all over again.
