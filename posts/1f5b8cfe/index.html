China Expands Visa-Free Access as Inbound Tourism Booms

Over the past few years, China has been opening its doors to the world at an unprecedented pace.

With the continuous expansion of unilateral visa-free policies, longer visa-free stays, simplified customs clearance procedures, improved multilingual services, and the rapid recovery of international flights, traveling to China has become easier than ever for global visitors.

As someone living in China, I can personally feel these changes happening very quickly.

In cities like Chongqing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Beijing, it has become increasingly common to see foreign tourists using mobile payment, taking high-speed rail, exploring night markets, recording cyberpunk cityscapes, and sharing their experiences on social media.

Many overseas visitors are surprised that modern China today feels very different from the stereotypes they once imagined.

China is no longer only associated with ancient landmarks and traditional culture.

Today, it is also:

  • One of the world’s largest high-speed rail networks
  • A country deeply integrated with AI and digital technology
  • Home to futuristic megacities and cyberpunk-style skylines
  • A place where ancient history and modern innovation coexist together

From Chongqing’s mountain cyberpunk atmosphere to Shenzhen’s futuristic tech ecosystem, from Beijing’s imperial history to Shanghai’s global urban culture, China is becoming one of the world’s most fascinating travel destinations for international visitors.

1. Latest China Visa-Free Policy

China’s visa-free policy has continued to expand rapidly since 2024, becoming one of the most discussed travel topics among overseas travelers.

For many foreign tourists, especially those from Europe, Asia, Oceania and South America, visiting China is now significantly easier and more convenient.

As a Chinese local, I have noticed that many foreign travelers are especially surprised by how smooth the actual entry process has become compared with what they expected before arriving.

  • Eligible Applicants: Holders of ordinary passports
  • Allowed Stay: Up to 30 days per entry

2. China’s Inbound Tourism Is Growing Rapidly

According to official data released by China’s National Immigration Administration, inbound tourism continued to grow rapidly throughout 2026.

More importantly, visa-free entry has now become the primary way many foreigners travel to China.

This is something very noticeable inside Chinese cities today.

Compared with several years ago, foreign travelers are now much easier to encounter in airports, subway systems, cafes, scenic areas, and even ordinary residential districts.

2.2 Why Foreign Tourists Suddenly Love China Again

As someone living in China, I think several changes are driving this trend.

First, modern China has become far more convenient for independent travel.

Foreign tourists can now:

  • Bind overseas bank cards to Alipay and WeChat Pay
  • Travel across provinces by high-speed rail
  • Use AI translation tools in real time
  • Book hotels and transportation online easily
  • Use mobile apps for food delivery and transportation

Second, social media has changed global perceptions.

Videos about Chongqing’s cyberpunk skyline, Chinese high-speed rail, robot restaurants, AI technology, drone shows and modern urban life are becoming increasingly viral on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram.

Many travelers are realizing that modern China feels very different from what they imagined before visiting.

3. Recommended Chinese Cities for Visa-Free Travelers

Chongqing — China’s Real-Life Cyberpunk City

As someone born and raised in Chongqing, I honestly believe this city is one of the most visually unique places in the world. I’ve lived here for over 20 years, and even today, I still find new corners that take my breath away—this is the magic of my hometown. Unlike most flat Chinese cities, Chongqing is built entirely on mountains and valleys, which means you’ll often find roads that twist up cliffs, buildings stacked on top of each other, and light rail lines that cut through residential skyscrapers. It’s not “designed” to be cyberpunk; it’s just how we’ve adapted to the land, and that’s what makes it feel so authentic.

Chongqing is chaotic, mountainous, crowded, futuristic, and deeply human at the same time. Let me break that down for you, as a local: the chaos comes from the endless streams of people on Jiefangbei Pedestrian Street, the honking of cars on narrow mountain roads, and the buzz of street food stalls that stay open until 2 AM. The mountains aren’t just a backdrop—they’re part of our daily lives. I grew up climbing stairs to get home, and even now, I still walk 10+ floors of steps some days instead of taking the elevator (it’s our version of exercise!). The futurism is in the neon lights that cover Hongyadong at night, the 24-hour convenience stores with cashless payment, and the way our light rail glides through buildings like something out of a sci-fi movie. But the humanity? That’s in the grandmas selling hot pot skewers on the street, the taxi drivers who will chat with you (in broken English, but with so much enthusiasm), and the way strangers will help you carry your luggage up a steep hill—Chongqing people are tough, but we’re also warm.

Many foreign travelers arrive expecting “another Chinese city,” but leave feeling completely shocked by the experience. I’ve met countless tourists at Liziba Station who stand there with their cameras, mouths open, watching the light rail pass through the building. They often ask me, “How did you build this?” and my answer is always the same: we built it for practicality, not for show. This city doesn’t try to be something it’s not—it’s unapologetically itself, and that’s why it resonates with so many visitors. As a local, I love seeing their reactions; it’s a reminder of how special my hometown really is.

Recommended Experiences

  • Hongyadong night view: As a local, I never get tired of this spot. Go after 8 PM when all the lights are on—you’ll see the traditional stilted buildings lit up like a fairy tale, reflecting on the Jialing River. Pro tip: Skip the crowded main platform; walk to the nearby Hongya Cave Riverside Road for a quieter view (and better photos). I often go there with friends on weekends to drink tea and watch the boats go by.

  • Liziba light rail station: This is the most famous spot in Chongqing for a reason, but as someone who takes this line occasionally, let me give you a hack: go on a weekday morning (9-11 AM) to avoid the crowds. The light rail passes through the building every 2-3 minutes, so you’ll have plenty of time to take photos. And if you’re feeling adventurous, take the train one stop to Niujiaotuo Station—you’ll get a great view of the city’s mountainous skyline.

  • Yangtze River Cableway: This isn’t just a tourist attraction for us locals—many people use it to commute to work! It’s been operating since 1987, so it has a lot of history. The ride takes about 5 minutes, and you’ll get a bird’s-eye view of the Yangtze River, the city’s skyscrapers, and the old neighborhoods along the banks. I used to take it to school when I was a kid, and it still feels like a little adventure every time.

  • Nanshan panoramic skyline: If you want to see the entire city at night, this is the place to go. As a local, I recommend going to the Nanshan Tree Viewing Platform (not the crowded main one)—it’s a 10-minute walk from the parking lot, but the view is worth it. You’ll see both the Yangtze and Jialing Rivers, Hongyadong, and the entire downtown area lit up. Bring a jacket—mountain nights can be cool, even in summer.

  • Local hotpot restaurants: Do not eat at the hotpot restaurants near tourist spots—they’re overpriced and not authentic. As a local, I go to small, family-run spots like “Old Town Hotpot” (Laocheng Hotpot) in Shapingba. Ask for “medium spicy” if you’re not used to Sichuan/Chongqing spice—our “mild” is spicier than most foreign travelers expect! And try the duck blood, lotus root slices, and beef rolls—they’re mandatory for a real Chongqing hotpot experience.

  • Riverside night walks: After dinner, locals love walking along the Jialing River Embankment. It’s a great way to cool down, watch the street performers, and see the city from a different angle. You’ll see families flying kites, couples taking photos, and elderly people dancing to traditional music. It’s a perfect way to experience the “human” side of Chongqing that tourists often miss.

Shenzhen — China’s Futuristic Technology Capital

If Chongqing feels like cyberpunk China, then Shenzhen feels like China’s future laboratory. I’ve visited Shenzhen almost every year for the past decade, and every time I go, I’m shocked by how much it’s changed. It’s a city that doesn’t stand still—new skyscrapers go up every month, new tech products are launched every week, and the pace of life is faster than any other city in China. As a Chinese person, Shenzhen feels like a window into our country’s future—it’s where innovation meets ambition, and it’s absolutely fascinating to witness.

Only a few decades ago, Shenzhen was still a small fishing town. My parents told me stories about visiting Shenzhen in the 1990s—back then, it was just a few streets and factories. Today, it has become one of the world’s leading technology cities. It’s home to companies like Huawei, Tencent, and DJI, and it’s where most of the world’s smartphones, drones, and smart devices are made. What I love most about Shenzhen is that it’s not just a “tech city”—it’s a city of immigrants. Over 80% of Shenzhen’s population comes from other parts of China, which means it’s diverse, open, and full of energy. You’ll hear people speaking different dialects, eating food from all over China, and chasing their dreams—this diversity is what makes Shenzhen so dynamic.

Walking through Shenzhen, you will constantly encounter:

  • EVs: Shenzhen was one of the first cities in China to replace all its taxis and public buses with electric vehicles. You’ll rarely see a gas-powered car on the roads, which makes the city much cleaner and quieter than other Chinese metropolises. As a local, I find it amazing how quickly Shenzhen adopted this change—It shows how the city is always looking forward.

  • Smart delivery robots: Go to any tech park or shopping mall in Shenzhen, and you’ll see robots delivering food, packages, and even coffee. They navigate the crowds seamlessly, and it’s not uncommon to see them interacting with people. I once saw a robot stop to let an elderly person cross the street—it’s small moments like that that show how tech in Shenzhen is designed to help people, not replace them.

  • AI companies: Shenzhen is a hub for AI startups—you’ll find small offices and large tech parks dedicated to AI research and development everywhere. Many of these companies are open to visitors (with an appointment), so if you’re a tech enthusiast, you can get a behind-the-scenes look at how AI is being used in everything from healthcare to transportation. I visited an AI company last year that was developing smart home systems, and it was incredible to see how they’re making daily life easier for people.

  • Futuristic skyscrapers: Shenzhen’s skyline is constantly evolving, but my favorite building is the Ping An Finance Center—it’s one of the tallest buildings in China, and its design is sleek and modern. The top floor has an observation deck with 360-degree views of the city, and it’s worth a visit just to see how the city is laid out. Another must-see is the DJI Sky City—it’s the headquarters of DJI, and its unique, futuristic design looks like something from a sci-fi movie.

  • Digital infrastructure everywhere: Shenzhen is the most digitalized city in China—you can do almost everything with your phone: pay for meals, take the subway, rent a bike, even enter buildings. I once forgot my wallet when I visited Shenzhen, and I didn’t have any problems—from breakfast to transportation to shopping, everything was cashless. It’s a level of convenience that even many Western cities haven’t achieved yet.

For many tech enthusiasts visiting China, Shenzhen is almost a must-see destination. But as a Chinese person, I think Shenzhen is more than just a tech hub—it’s a symbol of China’s growth and innovation. It shows how quickly our country can change, and how we’re embracing the future with open arms. Whether you’re into tech or not, Shenzhen will surprise you with its energy and ambition.

4. Why 2026 May Be the Best Time to Visit China

From my personal observation, modern China is currently in a very unique stage. I’ve lived in China my entire life, and I can honestly say that right now is the best time to visit—for reasons that I, as a local, can feel deeply. We’re in a sweet spot where the country still retains strong local culture and relatively affordable travel costs, while simultaneously becoming more international, more digitalized, and more traveler-friendly. It’s a balance that’s hard to find, and I don’t think it will last forever.

Let me explain what I mean. When I was a kid, traveling in China was much harder for foreigners—there were fewer English signs, cash was mandatory, and many places weren’t set up for international visitors. Today, things are completely different: most major cities have bilingual signs, almost every store and restaurant accepts foreign credit cards and mobile payment (WeChat Pay and Alipay now support overseas cards), and many attractions have English-speaking guides. But despite these changes, we haven’t lost our local culture. You can still find small, family-run restaurants serving traditional food, ancient neighborhoods that haven’t been gentrified, and festivals that are celebrated the same way they have been for centuries. This mix of modern convenience and traditional charm is what makes China so special right now.

Another reason 2026 is the perfect time is the visa-free policy. As a local, I’ve noticed a huge increase in foreign travelers since the policy was extended and expanded. It’s amazing to see people from all over the world walking the streets of my hometown, trying our food, and asking questions about our culture. The Chinese government has made a lot of effort to make China more accessible to foreign visitors, and 2026 is when those efforts are really paying off. You can come to China without the hassle of applying for a visa, stay for 30 days, and experience everything this country has to offer.

This combination may not last forever. As China continues to develop, travel costs will likely go up, and some of the smaller, more authentic neighborhoods may be replaced by modern buildings. I’ve already seen this happen in some parts of Shenzhen and Shanghai—old alleyways that I used to visit as a kid are now skyscrapers. That’s not a bad thing, but it means that the China of today is a unique snapshot in time. That is why I believe now is one of the most fascinating periods to experience China.

As a local, I also want to add this: 2026 is a great year to visit because the country is in a positive, optimistic mood. We’re celebrating the progress we’ve made, and we’re excited to share our culture with the world. You’ll feel that warmth and enthusiasm when you talk to locals—we love showing visitors around, answering their questions, and hearing about their home countries. It’s a two-way exchange that makes travel meaningful, and it’s something you won’t get from just reading about China online.

5. Final Thoughts

China today is much more than many people imagine. I’ve met so many foreign travelers who come to China with stereotypes—they think it’s all skyscrapers and factories, or all ancient temples and traditional villages. But the truth is, it’s both. It is ancient yet futuristic. Traditional yet highly digitalized. Chaotic yet incredibly efficient. That’s the beauty of China—it’s not one thing; it’s a mix of contradictions that somehow work together perfectly.

And perhaps the most interesting part is this: China is changing so quickly that even people living here constantly feel surprised by it. I’ve lived in Chongqing for over 20 years, and every year, there’s something new— a new light rail line, a new park, a new restaurant. It’s exciting to be part of this change, and it’s even more exciting to share it with foreign visitors. I often tell my foreign friends that you can’t visit China once and see everything—you have to come back again and again to keep up with how much it’s changing.

As someone writing from Chongqing, I simply hope more overseas travelers can experience this side of China themselves instead of only seeing it through stereotypes online. The media often portrays China in a one-dimensional way—either as a tech giant or a traditional country—but the real China is so much more nuanced. It’s the grandma selling hot pot skewers on the street, the tech worker rushing to a meeting in Shenzhen, the farmer harvesting rice in a small village, and the student practicing English in a café. It’s the little moments, the people, and the culture that make China truly special.

Because the real China is often far more interesting than people expect. It’s a country that’s full of surprises, warmth, and energy. And in 2026, with the visa-free policy making it easier than ever to visit, there’s no better time to come and see it for yourself. As a local, I’ll be here, ready to welcome you—and I promise, you won’t be disappointed.
