Eurovision 2026: 70 Years of Music, Politics, and Controversy

The Eurovision Song Contest is turning 70 — and Eurovision 2026 is already shaping up to be one of the most talked-about editions in the event’s history. As a music and culture blogger based in Shanghai, China, I’ve watched Eurovision casually for years, but this anniversary edition has captured my full attention — and I’m not alone. Among Chinese music fans, especially those who love European pop, indie, and electronic music, Eurovision 2026 is already a hot topic on social platforms like Douyin, Weibo, and Bilibili. What was once a niche European event for Chinese audiences has grown into a must-watch, thanks to viral performances, online discussions, and the sheer scale of this year’s 70th celebration.

Hosted in Vienna, Austria, this year’s competition is attracting attention far beyond the music world. Search interest has surged more than 900%, driven by political controversy, voting rule changes, returning countries, and massive anniversary celebrations. In China, I’ve noticed this surge firsthand: my social media feeds are filled with discussions about the boycott, predictions for the winner, and clips of past Eurovision classics. For Chinese viewers like me, Eurovision is no longer just a “European singing contest” — it’s a window into European culture, politics, and artistic expression, wrapped in the glitz and drama that makes the event so addictive.

For some viewers, Eurovision remains the world’s biggest music party. For others, it has become a reflection of Europe’s political tensions. Either way, Eurovision 2026 is impossible to ignore — even for those of us thousands of miles away in China, who tune in at 3 AM local time to catch the live shows, armed with coffee and enthusiasm.

What Is Eurovision 2026?

The Eurovision Song Contest 2026 marks the 70th edition of the annual competition organized by the European Broadcasting Union (EBU). For Chinese audiences, understanding Eurovision can be a bit confusing at first — it’s not just a singing competition, but a cultural institution with decades of history, unwritten rules, and a passionate global fanbase. When I first started watching Eurovision a few years ago, I had to learn the basics: the “big five” countries (UK, France, Germany, Italy, Spain) that automatically qualify for the final, the semi-final format, and the iconic 12-point voting system. Now, it’s a tradition for me and many Chinese Eurovision fans to gather online, live-comment during the shows, and debate the best performances.

Austria earned the right to host after singer JJ won Eurovision 2025 with the song Wasted Love. For Chinese fans, JJ’s victory was a standout moment — her powerful vocals and emotional performance went viral on Douyin, with many Chinese music lovers covering her song and sharing reaction videos. Vienna — often called the “City of Music” — was selected as the host city for the third time after previously staging the contest in 1967 and 2015. As someone who loves classical music, I know Vienna for its connection to Mozart and Beethoven, so seeing it host Eurovision feels like a perfect blend of Europe’s musical past and present.

The main venue will once again be the Wiener Stadthalle, one of Austria’s largest indoor arenas. For Chinese fans who dream of attending in person, the venue’s capacity and history add to the excitement — though most of us will have to settle for streaming the shows, which is easier than ever thanks to the official Eurovision app and Chinese streaming platforms that now carry the event.

Important Dates

  • Opening Ceremony: May 10, 2026

  • Semi-Final 1: May 12, 2026

  • Semi-Final 2: May 14, 2026

  • Grand Final: May 16, 2026

All live shows are scheduled for 21:00 CEST — which translates to 3:00 AM Beijing time. For Chinese fans, this means late nights (or early mornings), but it’s a sacrifice we’re willing to make. I’ve already set my alarm and planned a virtual watch party with friends from across China who share my love for Eurovision. We’ll chat online, react to the performances, and debate the voting results, turning a European event into a shared Chinese fan experience.

According to organizers, more than 150 million viewers worldwide are expected to watch the event, maintaining Eurovision’s position as one of the most-watched non-sporting television broadcasts on Earth. I have no doubt that Chinese viewers will make up a significant portion of that number — our community is small but growing, and this 70th anniversary has only fueled our enthusiasm.

Why Eurovision 2026 Is Suddenly Everywhere

Several major developments pushed Eurovision 2026 into global trending charts. For Chinese fans, the buzz started with the 70th anniversary — nostalgia is a universal emotion, and even though we didn’t grow up watching Eurovision, we’ve fallen in love with its history through retrospective clips and documentaries shared online. I’ve spent hours on Bilibili watching classic Eurovision performances from the 1970s and 1980s, fascinated by how the contest has evolved over the decades, from cheesy pop to experimental electronic music and everything in between.

The biggest reason is simple: this is the contest’s 70th anniversary. Eurovision has existed since 1956, and generations of fans across Europe grew up watching it. The anniversary alone created huge nostalgia-driven attention online. In China, this nostalgia has translated into curiosity — we’re eager to learn more about the contest’s history, its most iconic moments, and the artists who have shaped it. Social media hashtags like #Eurovision70 and #Eurovision2026 have gained traction on Weibo, with thousands of Chinese fans sharing their favorite Eurovision memories and predictions.

But politics turned the competition into a much larger global story — and it’s a story that has resonated deeply with Chinese audiences. We’re no strangers to political tensions and their impact on cultural events, so the boycott by five European countries felt familiar, even if the specifics are different. The debate over whether cultural events should be “politically neutral” is one that Chinese fans have discussed at length online, with many sharing their own perspectives on where art and politics intersect.

Five countries — Spain, Ireland, the Netherlands, Iceland, and Slovenia — announced boycotts over Israel’s participation in the contest during the ongoing Gaza conflict. That decision triggered intense debate across Europe and social media platforms worldwide. In China, this controversy dominated our Eurovision discussions: some fans agreed with the boycott, arguing that human rights should take precedence over entertainment, while others believed Eurovision should remain a space for music, not politics. The divide mirrors global opinions, but it’s been fascinating to see Chinese fans engage with these complex issues, drawing on our own cultural context to form opinions.

At the same time, Eurovision introduced major voting reforms after criticism surrounding the 2025 results, especially allegations of organized online voting campaigns. For Chinese fans, this was a relatable issue — we’ve seen similar problems in Chinese talent shows and online voting events, so we understand the frustration of unfair manipulation. The reforms have sparked hope among Chinese Eurovision fans that this year’s results will be more fair and reflective of genuine fan sentiment.

Together, those three factors — the 70th anniversary, political controversy, and voting reforms — transformed Eurovision 2026 from a music event into a major international conversation. For Chinese fans, it’s no longer just about watching singing performances; it’s about engaging with global culture, politics, and community.

The Biggest Controversy: Five Countries Boycott Eurovision

Eurovision has experienced political disputes before, but never on this scale. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve followed the controversy closely, and it’s clear that this is more than just a disagreement over participation — it’s a reflection of deeper global tensions. In China, we’re used to seeing cultural events become caught up in politics, but Eurovision’s boycott feels different because of the contest’s reputation as a “unifying” event.

Five participating nations withdrew from the 2026 competition in protest against Israel remaining in the contest. Demonstrations quickly appeared in Vienna, with protesters accusing the EBU of ignoring humanitarian concerns linked to the Gaza war. In China, videos of these demonstrations have been shared widely on social media, with many Chinese fans expressing sympathy for the protesters, while others criticized the boycott as “politicizing music.”

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez publicly supported the boycott, adding even more attention to the issue. Social media videos discussing Eurovision’s political divisions gained millions of views within days — including in China, where users on Douyin and Weibo shared clips and debated the ethics of Israel’s participation. I’ve seen heated discussions in Chinese fan groups, with some arguing that the EBU should prioritize human rights, while others argued that excluding Israel punishes artists who have nothing to do with the conflict.

The EBU defended its decision, arguing that Israel still met Eurovision’s participation requirements. Israeli officials also rejected accusations connected to the conflict. For Chinese fans, this back-and-forth feels familiar — it’s a common debate in global cultural events: where do you draw the line between politics and art?

The controversy has divided fans. In China, our Eurovision community is split, too. Some of my friends who watch Eurovision purely for the music are disappointed by the boycott, saying it takes away from the joy of the event. Others, however, believe that staying silent on political issues is a form of complicity. It’s a nuanced debate, and one that has made our Eurovision discussions more meaningful — we’re not just talking about songs; we’re talking about values, justice, and the role of culture in society.

Some believe Eurovision should remain politically neutral and focus only on music. Others argue the contest has always reflected Europe’s political climate, whether organizers admit it or not. As someone who has watched Eurovision for years, I tend to agree with the latter — even in the most “apolitical” years, the contest is a reflection of European identity, unity, and division. The 2026 boycott is just a more explicit example of that.

Regardless of opinion, the boycott has already become one of the defining moments in Eurovision history. For Chinese fans, it’s a reminder that music is never truly “neutral” — it’s a product of the world we live in, and it has the power to spark important conversations.

Eurovision 2026 Will Have 35 Participating Countries

Despite the withdrawals, Eurovision 2026 will still feature 35 countries. For Chinese fans, this number is significant — it means a diverse lineup of music, from pop and rock to traditional folk and electronic, representing different cultures and languages. One of the things I love most about Eurovision is discovering music from countries I know little about; it’s a way to learn about European culture without leaving my home in Shanghai.

Interestingly, several nations are returning to the competition:

  • Bulgaria

  • Moldova

  • Romania

Their return adds fresh energy to the lineup and partially offsets the impact of the boycott. For Chinese fans, this is exciting news — Bulgaria has produced some of our favorite Eurovision entries in recent years, with their catchy pop songs and visually stunning performances. Moldova, too, is a fan favorite among Chinese viewers, thanks to their quirky, energetic performances that often go viral online. I’ve already seen Chinese fans sharing past Moldova entries on Douyin, expressing excitement for their return in 2026.

Still, 35 participants marks Eurovision’s smallest field since 2003, highlighting how unusual this year’s contest has become. For Chinese fans, though, the number of participants doesn’t matter as much as the quality of the music and the drama of the event. Even with fewer countries, we’re expecting a memorable show — especially with the 70th anniversary celebrations adding extra glitz and emotion.

New Voting Rules Aim to Prevent Manipulation

After heavy criticism in 2025, Eurovision organizers introduced several important rule changes. For Chinese fans, this is a welcome development — we’ve all experienced the frustration of unfair voting in talent shows, whether it’s in China or abroad. The 2025 Eurovision results sparked controversy worldwide, with allegations of organized online voting campaigns skewing the outcome. In China, we discussed these allegations at length in our fan groups, with many of us hoping for reforms to make the voting more fair.

The goal is clear: restore trust in the voting system. For Chinese Eurovision fans, trust is key — we want to feel like the winner is truly the most popular or most talented artist, not someone who benefited from coordinated voting. These rule changes give us hope that this year’s results will be more legitimate.

Jury Votes Return to Semi-Finals

For the past few years, semi-finals relied entirely on public voting. In 2026, professional juries will return alongside televoters. This means qualification decisions will once again use a mixed system. For Chinese fans, this is a smart move — public voting can be unpredictable (and sometimes manipulated), but juries bring a level of expertise and objectivity. We’ve seen similar systems work well in Chinese music competitions, where a mix of jury and public votes balances artistic merit with fan preference.

Maximum Votes Reduced

Previously, viewers could cast up to 20 votes. Now, the limit drops to 10 votes per person or payment method. Organizers hope this reduces coordinated voting campaigns and artificial influence. For Chinese fans, this makes sense — allowing fewer votes per person prevents groups from flooding the system with votes for a single artist. It levels the playing field and ensures that each vote counts more.

Larger Professional Juries

National juries are expanding from 5 members to 7 members. The EBU also introduced diversity requirements, including younger jurors and wider age gaps between members. For Chinese fans, this diversity is important — it ensures that the jury represents a range of musical tastes and perspectives, not just a small group of industry insiders. We’ve seen how a lack of diversity in juries can lead to biased results in Chinese competitions, so this reform is a step in the right direction.

Stricter Promotion Rules

Eurovision 2026 will crack down on excessive online promotion campaigns, especially those backed by governments or political organizations. This rule appears directly connected to criticism surrounding Israel’s strong public vote performance in 2025. For Chinese fans, this is a crucial reform — we’ve seen how government-backed promotion can skew results in other international events, so limiting this practice will make the competition more fair. It also ensures that the focus remains on the music, not on political or financial influence.

Vienna Is Turning Eurovision Into a City-Wide Festival

Eurovision is never just about the arena shows — and this year, Vienna is taking that to the next level. For Chinese fans, this city-wide celebration is one of the most exciting parts of Eurovision 2026. Even though we can’t be there in person, we’re living vicariously through social media updates, live streams from the Eurovision Village, and photos of Vienna decorated for the occasion.

For the 70th anniversary, Vienna plans to transform the entire city into a giant celebration zone. As someone who has always wanted to visit Vienna, seeing the city embrace Eurovision makes me even more eager to travel there someday. The blend of classical music history and modern pop culture is fascinating, and it perfectly captures what makes Eurovision so special: it’s a celebration of both tradition and innovation.

The Rathausplatz area will become the official Eurovision Village, featuring:

  • Public screenings

  • Live performances

  • Fan events

  • Outdoor concerts

  • Cultural exhibitions

For Chinese fans, the Eurovision Village is a window into European fan culture. We’ve seen clips of past Eurovision Villages online, with fans from all over Europe singing, dancing, and celebrating together. It’s a reminder of the unifying power of music — even across language and cultural barriers. I’ve seen Chinese fans express envy of those who can attend, but we’re also finding ways to celebrate at home: hosting our own watch parties, decorating our spaces with Eurovision-themed decor, and sharing our excitement online.

Meanwhile, the Euro Club will host after-parties, artist appearances, and media events throughout Eurovision week. For Chinese fans who follow their favorite Eurovision artists on social media, this means more content to enjoy — behind-the-scenes clips, interviews, and impromptu performances that give us a closer look at the artists we love.

Austria is clearly treating Eurovision 2026 as both a cultural celebration and a tourism opportunity. For Chinese travelers, this could be a chance to visit Vienna and experience Eurovision firsthand — though tickets and travel plans are already in high demand. Even if we can’t go, seeing Vienna embrace the contest makes us feel more connected to the event, even from thousands of miles away.

A New Eurovision Archive and Documentary

To celebrate seven decades of Eurovision history, organizers are launching several anniversary projects. For Chinese fans, these projects are a goldmine — they allow us to catch up on the contest’s rich history, which we missed out on growing up. I’ve already marked my calendar to explore the new digital archive, as I’m eager to learn more about Eurovision’s early years and the artists who shaped it.

The official Eurovision platform will introduce the “Eurodex,” a large digital archive covering past contests, artists, rankings, and memorable performances. For Chinese fans, this is a game-changer — previously, it was hard to find high-quality clips of old Eurovision performances in China, but the Eurodex will make this history accessible to everyone. I plan to spend hours browsing the archive, watching classic performances and learning about the contest’s evolution over the past 70 years.

A new documentary film titled Life of Toni is also in development. According to early reports, the film explores Eurovision’s emotional and cultural impact through the experiences of long-time fans. For Chinese fans, this documentary will be a chance to connect with the global Eurovision community — to see how the contest has touched people’s lives around the world. I’m especially excited to hear from fans who have watched Eurovision for decades, as their stories will give me a deeper understanding of the contest’s significance.

Several books and retrospective projects are also expected ahead of the Grand Final. For Chinese fans who love reading and learning about music culture, these books will be a great way to dive deeper into Eurovision’s history. I’ve already seen Chinese bookstores promoting upcoming Eurovision books, which is a sign of how much the contest has grown in popularity in China.

Why Eurovision Still Matters After 70 Years

Eurovision survives because it constantly evolves. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve watched this evolution firsthand — from a niche European event to a global cultural phenomenon that resonates with fans in China and beyond. What started as a post-war European television experiment became a global cultural phenomenon. Today, the contest mixes music, internet culture, national identity, politics, and fandom into one massive live event — and that’s exactly why it appeals to Chinese fans.

For younger audiences in China, Eurovision is often chaotic, ironic, and unpredictable. We love the over-the-top performances, the catchy songs, and the drama of the voting. It’s a welcome break from the polished, often formulaic pop music that dominates Chinese airwaves. Eurovision’s quirkiness and diversity feel fresh and exciting, and it allows us to discover new artists and musical styles we might never have encountered otherwise.

For older viewers in China — those who grew up listening to European classical music and pop — Eurovision represents decades of shared television history. Many of my friends’ parents, who love classical music, have become interested in Eurovision because of its connection to Vienna and European music culture. It’s a way for them to connect with their own love of music, while also discovering new artists.

And despite constant criticism, the contest continues growing. In China, our Eurovision fan community is getting bigger every year. More and more Chinese music fans are discovering the contest through social media, viral clips, and word of mouth. We’re creating fan groups, sharing content, and even traveling to Europe to attend Eurovision in person. It’s a testament to the contest’s universal appeal — music truly transcends borders.

That may be the most remarkable thing about Eurovision: every year people claim it has become “too political” or “too strange,” yet millions keep watching. For Chinese fans, this resilience is inspiring. In a world that’s often divided, Eurovision brings people together — whether they’re in Vienna, Shanghai, or anywhere else in the world. It’s a reminder that music has the power to unite us, even when everything else seems to divide us.

How to Watch Eurovision 2026

Eurovision 2026 will air live across participating countries and many international markets, including:

  • United States

  • Canada

  • Australia

For Chinese fans, watching Eurovision has never been easier. The official Eurovision website and mobile app will stream all shows live, and several Chinese streaming platforms have also secured the rights to broadcast the event. I’ve already downloaded the Eurovision app and set up notifications so I don’t miss a single performance. For those who can’t stay up until 3 AM, many Chinese platforms will also offer on-demand replays the next day, so we can catch up at our convenience.

In-person tickets for rehearsals, semi-finals, and the Grand Final are expected to sell quickly, especially with Vienna hosting the anniversary edition. A few of my Chinese friends are planning to travel to Vienna to attend the show — they’ve been saving money for months, and I’m incredibly jealous. For the rest of us, though, streaming the event and joining virtual fan parties is the next best thing.

Organizers estimate that hundreds of thousands of visitors will travel to Austria during Eurovision week. I have no doubt that a small but dedicated group of Chinese fans will be among them, representing our country and our love for Eurovision. It’s a chance to connect with the global fan community and experience the magic of Eurovision in person — something many of us have dreamed of for years.

Final Thoughts

Eurovision 2026 feels bigger than a normal song contest. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve never seen so much excitement around Eurovision in China — it’s no longer a niche event; it’s a cultural moment that has captured the attention of music fans across our country. The combination of political tension, anniversary celebrations, voting reforms, and global attention has turned this year’s edition into a cultural moment far beyond Europe’s music scene.

Some viewers will tune in for the performances. For Chinese fans like me, this is the heart of Eurovision — discovering new music, falling in love with catchy songs, and being entertained by the over-the-top performances. We’ll cheer for our favorite artists, debate the best entries, and share our excitement with friends online.

Others will watch for the controversy. The boycott and political debates have added a layer of drama that makes Eurovision 2026 even more compelling. For Chinese fans, these debates are a chance to engage with global issues, share our perspectives, and learn from people with different opinions.

But almost everyone expects one thing: Eurovision 2026 will be unforgettable. For Chinese fans, this 70th anniversary edition is more than just a singing contest — it’s a chance to connect with global culture, make new friends, and celebrate the power of music. Whether we’re watching from our homes in Shanghai, Beijing, or Guangzhou, we’ll be part of a global community, united by our love for Eurovision. And that’s the magic of this event: it brings people together, no matter where they’re from, no matter what language they speak, and no matter what their political beliefs are. Here’s to 70 years of Eurovision — and to many more.
