Why Chongqing Feels Like a Real-Life Cyberpunk City

Created2026-05-15|Updated2026-05-20|Travel

Why Chongqing Feels Like a Real-Life Cyberpunk City

Before I came to Chongqing, the word “cyberpunk” only existed in movies for me. As a travel blogger born and raised in Chengdu, China—just a 2-hour high-speed train ride from Chongqing—I’d heard countless stories about this “mountain city” from friends and family. They described it as “chaotic yet charming,” “vertical and confusing,” but never once did I connect it to the cyberpunk aesthetic I’d grown to love in sci-fi films. Growing up in a relatively flat city, I couldn’t fathom how a place could feel both ancient and futuristic, both gritty and vibrant, all at once.

It was the rainy skyscrapers of Blade Runner, the neon alleys of Ghost in the Shell, and the dystopian atmosphere of The Matrix. I’d rewatched those films dozens of times, admiring their futuristic visuals but never thinking I’d experience anything like that in real life—let alone in my home country, China. To me, cyberpunk was a foreign, fictional concept, far removed from the bustling, traditional cities I’d visited across China.

But the moment I stood inside a crowded elevator near Jiefangbei and looked up at a monorail gliding between buildings above my head, while scooters rushed through steaming alleyways full of hotpot restaurants below, I suddenly understood something: As a Chinese local, I’m used to cities with distinct “districts” and logical layouts—Beijing’s wide avenues, Shanghai’s sleek skyline, Chengdu’s lazy pace—but Chongqing shattered all those expectations. It wasn’t just a “different” Chinese city; it was a world unto itself.

Chongqing is not like a cyberpunk city.

It is cyberpunk — just in real life. And as someone who has traveled to nearly every major city in China, I can say with certainty: there’s nothing else like it in the world, let alone in my home country.

A City Built in Vertical Layers

Most cities are horizontal. You walk along streets, move from one neighborhood to the next on flat ground, and GPS works (for the most part). Even hilly cities like Chengdu have gentle slopes, not the extreme verticality of Chongqing. As a Chinese local, I’ve never experienced a city that defies basic urban logic the way Chongqing does—it’s like someone took a normal city and stacked it on top of itself, then shook it up.

Chongqing is vertical.

Roads overlap roads. Buildings grow out of cliffs. Bridges hang over rivers like giant steel dragons. And entire neighborhoods exist on different elevations at the same time. For a local traveler used to flat cities, my first day in Chongqing left me disoriented—yet exhilarated. I’d walk what I thought was “straight” only to find myself three floors higher than I started, or turn a corner and face a staircase that seemed to lead to the sky.

In Chongqing:

  • The 11th floor can open directly onto a street. I stayed in a hotel near Ciqikou Ancient Town where the 11th-floor lobby opened onto a busy road—when I stepped outside, I was at eye level with trees and streetlights, while the hotel’s lower floors extended down the hillside like a waterfall. It felt like living in a sci-fi movie set, not a real Chinese city.

  • The 1st floor can also open onto another street. My local friend explained that this is called “multi-level street access,” a necessity in a city where flat land is a luxury. I once walked out of a convenience store on the 1st floor and found myself on a street that was 10 meters below the “main” road I’d been on 10 minutes earlier.

  • You might walk “downstairs” and accidentally arrive eight floors lower. I learned this the hard way when I tried to walk from a rooftop bar to a hotpot restaurant nearby. I followed a “downstairs” sign, walked for five minutes, and ended up in a completely different neighborhood—eight floors below, and a 20-minute walk from where I intended to be. My local friend laughed and said, “Welcome to Chongqing—GPS is just a suggestion here.”

  • GPS navigation regularly loses its mind. As a Chinese traveler who relies heavily on apps like Gaode Map (China’s equivalent of Google Maps) and WeChat Maps, I was shocked to find that even the most advanced navigation apps struggle in Chongqing. The city’s vertical layout, overlapping roads, and hidden alleyways make it nearly impossible for GPS to keep up. Locals don’t even bother with GPS—they use landmarks, or simply ask for directions (and trust me, Chongqing locals are some of the friendliest people in China, always happy to help a lost traveler).

This is why people call Chongqing an “8D Magic City.” The nickname, coined by Chinese netizens, perfectly captures the city’s surreal, multi-dimensional feel. It’s not just a marketing term—it’s a lived experience. For locals, this vertical chaos is normal; for someone like me, visiting from a flat city, it’s like stepping into a different reality.

The city feels less like urban planning and more like someone stacked multiple versions of reality on top of each other. It’s messy, it’s unorganized, and it’s absolutely fascinating. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve visited many “unique” cities in China, but none have made me feel like I’m inside a video game quite like Chongqing.

From the ground you see highways.

Above the highways: restaurants with outdoor patios, where diners eat hotpot while watching cars pass below.

Above the restaurants: apartments, with laundry hanging from balconies that overlook the streets far below.

Above the apartments: libraries, offices, rooftop bars, and more highways. I visited a rooftop bar in Yuzhong District where I could see three different levels of roads, two rivers, and dozens of skyscrapers—all from one spot. It felt like a giant playable map from a futuristic video game, and I couldn’t stop taking photos to share with my followers back home.

The Train That Runs Through a Building

Nothing represents Chongqing’s cyberpunk reputation better than Liziba Station. As a Chinese traveler, I’d seen photos and videos of this iconic station online—on Douyin, Weibo, and even international platforms like TikTok—but nothing prepared me for seeing it in person. It’s one of those things that sounds absurd until you’re standing there, watching a train pass through a residential building like it’s the most normal thing in the world.

Yes — the train literally passes through a residential building. Not just near it, not just next to it—through the middle of it. The station is built into the 8th to 10th floors of a 19-story apartment building, with the train entering one side, stopping for passengers, and exiting the other. For a local used to traditional Chinese infrastructure, it’s a engineering marvel; for a cyberpunk fan, it’s a dream come true.

When I arrived there at night, every passenger inside the metro pressed against the windows with phones in hand. Including me. I’d seen countless videos, but watching the train approach the building—its lights glowing in the dark, the sound of its wheels echoing through the neighborhood—felt surreal. It was like watching a scene from Blade Runner, but instead of actors and special effects, it was real people, going about their daily lives.

Outside the glass, I could see laundry hanging on balconies only meters away. A child jumping in a living room. Someone cooking dinner. I even saw an elderly woman watering her potted plants on her balcony, completely unfazed by the train passing just a few feet away. That’s the magic of Chongqing: the impossible becomes ordinary, and the surreal becomes part of daily life.

Then the train disappeared into the building itself. For a second, reality stopped making sense. I found myself holding my breath, half-expecting the train to crash or the building to shake—but nothing happened. It just emerged on the other side, continuing its journey, while the residents inside the building went about their evening routines.

But somehow, Chongqing makes impossible things feel normal. My local friend, who grew up near Liziba Station, told me that she doesn’t even notice the train anymore. “It’s just part of living here,” she said. “When I was a kid, I’d wave at the passengers from my balcony. Now, it’s like having a neighbor who passes by every few minutes.”

What sounds absurd elsewhere becomes practical here because of the mountain terrain. Chongqing is surrounded by mountains and crisscrossed by rivers—flat land is scarce, so locals and engineers had to get creative. The Liziba Station wasn’t built to be a tourist attraction; it was built out of necessity. It’s a brilliant solution to a geographic problem, and it’s become a symbol of Chongqing’s ingenuity.

And after operating safely for more than 20 years, it has become one of the most iconic urban scenes in China. It’s not just a station—it’s a cultural landmark, a symbol of Chongqing’s ability to turn limitations into strengths. For Chinese travelers, it’s a must-visit spot; for foreign visitors, it’s often the first thing they think of when they hear “Chongqing.”

The Infrastructure Feels Like Science Fiction

Cyberpunk is not only about neon lights. It is also about infrastructure overwhelming human perception—giant, imposing structures that make you feel small, a blend of technology and nature that feels both futuristic and gritty. And Chongqing does that perfectly. As a Chinese blogger who has written about infrastructure across China, I can say that no other city in my country comes close to Chongqing’s scale and innovation.

The city has:

  • Over 20,000 bridges. That’s more than any other city in China—and possibly the world. Chongqing is called the “Bridge Capital of China,” and it’s easy to see why. Everywhere you look, there are bridges: suspension bridges, arch bridges, cable-stayed bridges, even bridges that connect different levels of the city. I took a boat tour on the Yangtze River one evening and counted 12 bridges in just 30 minutes—each one more impressive than the last.

  • Multi-level highways twisting through mountains. These highways don’t just go around mountains—they cut through them, wrap around them, and even go over them. I drove from Jiangbei Airport to my hotel in Yuzhong District, and the highway felt like a roller coaster: ascending steeply, descending sharply, and twisting around mountain peaks. It was equal parts terrifying and thrilling, and it felt like driving through a sci-fi movie.

  • Massive layered overpasses. These overpasses have multiple levels, with cars driving on top of each other, and they’re so complex that even locals sometimes get lost. I once asked a taxi driver how he navigates them, and he laughed and said, “You learn by mistake. After a few wrong exits, you remember the way.”

  • Metro systems built into cliffs. Chongqing’s metro isn’t just underground—it’s built into the sides of mountains, with stations that open onto cliffs and rivers. One station, Chaotianmen, is built right on the banks of the Yangtze River, with floor-to-ceiling windows that offer stunning views of the water and the city skyline. It’s like riding a train through a postcard.

  • Endless tunnels cutting through hills. Chongqing has more tunnels than any other city in China—over 1,000, by some estimates. These tunnels are long, dark, and often curved, and they add to the city’s cyberpunk vibe. Driving through them feels like entering a different world, with the only light coming from the car’s headlights and the glow of exit signs.

The most famous example is Huangjuewan Interchange. If you’ve seen any cyberpunk-style photos of Chongqing, you’ve probably seen this interchange. It’s a massive, 5-level structure that looks like it was lifted straight out of a sci-fi film.

It has:

  • 5 levels

  • 20 ramps

  • 8 directions

Even experienced drivers sometimes take the wrong exit there. My local friend told me that she once got lost on Huangjuewan Interchange and ended up driving in circles for 40 minutes. “It’s like a maze,” she said. “But once you learn it, it’s kind of fun.” For a visitor, it’s overwhelming—but in the best way possible. It’s a testament to Chongqing’s engineering prowess, and it’s one of the most visually striking infrastructure projects I’ve ever seen.

From above, it looks almost unreal — like a circuit board floating over the city. I took a drone photo of it (with permission, of course—drone laws are strict in Chinese cities) and posted it on my social media. My followers, both Chinese and international, couldn’t believe it was real. They thought it was a CGI render, but it’s just another day in Chongqing.

Unlike cities built on flat land, Chongqing had no choice but to innovate vertically. It couldn’t expand outward, so it expanded upward—and downward. And somehow, those geographic limitations became its greatest visual identity. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve come to appreciate this: Chongqing didn’t just adapt to its environment; it embraced it, turning a disadvantage into a unique selling point that attracts millions of visitors every year.

Chongqing at Night Feels Different

Many cities are beautiful at night. Shanghai’s Bund glows with neon and lights, Beijing’s Forbidden City is lit up in golden light, and Chengdu’s Chunxi Road is bustling with people. But Chongqing feels alive at night—in a way that no other Chinese city does. It’s not just beautiful; it’s immersive, overwhelming, and full of energy. As a local traveler, I’ve never experienced a city that transforms so dramatically after dark.

I went to TestBed 2, a renovated factory district built into the hillside. This area used to be an old factory, but it’s been turned into a trendy neighborhood with bars, restaurants, and art galleries—all while preserving the industrial aesthetic that makes it feel cyberpunk. It’s a perfect blend of old and new, which is exactly what makes Chongqing so special.

On one side: the smell of fried chicken. Not the chain restaurants you find in most Chinese cities, but local street food—spicy, crispy, and full of flavor. I bought a portion from a street vendor and ate it while walking, watching the crowds pass by.

On another: a guitarist singing in a small bar. The bar was tiny, with only a few tables, but the guitarist’s voice was powerful, singing a mix of Chinese pop songs and Western classics. A group of locals sat nearby, singing along and clinking beer glasses. It was a warm, intimate moment, a stark contrast to the futuristic visuals of the city outside.

Ahead: the glowing skyline of Yuzhong District. The skyscrapers were lit up with neon lights, their reflections dancing on the Yangtze River below. The skyline was a mix of modern high-rises and older buildings, all stacked on top of each other, creating a visual feast that felt like it was straight out of a cyberpunk movie.

Behind: old walls covered with handwritten love messages. Locals and visitors alike had written messages on the walls—confessions of love, wishes for the future, even just random thoughts. It was a human touch in a city that sometimes feels like it’s from another world.

The city lights here do not feel clean or futuristic in a sterile way. They feel humid. Dense. Heavy. Human. Chongqing’s climate is hot and humid year-round, and that humidity hangs in the air at night, making the lights look softer, more diffused. It’s not the cold, sharp neon of Blade Runner; it’s warm, inviting, and full of life.

The air itself seems filled with steam, traffic noise, hotpot smoke, neon reflections, and river fog. I took a walk along the riverfront at night, and the air was thick with the smell of hotpot—spicy, savory, and impossible to ignore. It’s a smell that’s uniquely Chongqing, and it’s one that stays with you long after you leave.

That is what makes Chongqing different from Silicon Valley-style futurism. Its cyberpunk feeling is not “cold technology.” It is “hot humanity.” Silicon Valley’s futurism is sleek, clean, and impersonal—all glass and steel and robots. Chongqing’s futurism is messy, warm, and human—full of street food, laughter, and the chaos of daily life. As a Chinese blogger, I find this much more relatable. It’s not a future where humans are replaced by technology; it’s a future where humans and technology coexist, in all their messy, wonderful glory.

Hotpot Is the Language of Chongqing

If infrastructure is the skeleton of Chongqing, hotpot is its soul. As a Chinese person, I’ve eaten hotpot in many cities—Chengdu, Beijing, Shanghai—but nothing compares to Chongqing hotpot. It’s not just food; it’s a way of life, a cultural tradition, and a language that connects everyone in the city. For locals, hotpot is more than a meal—it’s a social event, a way to bond with friends and family, and a way to cope with the city’s hot, humid weather.

I walked into a random hotpot restaurant near Guanyinqiao—one of Chongqing’s busiest shopping districts—and ordered a small spicy beef tallow pot for myself. Guanyinqiao is full of hotpot restaurants, but I wanted something authentic, not a tourist trap. The restaurant was small, with plastic stools and tables, and the walls were covered with photos of locals eating hotpot. It felt like I’d stumbled into someone’s home.

The soup was dark red. Almost black. Chongqing hotpot is famous for its spicy beef tallow base, which is made with chili, Sichuan peppercorn, and other spices. It’s not just spicy—it’s numbing, savory, and full of flavor. As someone who loves spicy food (growing up in Chengdu, I’m no stranger to heat), I was excited to try it—but I wasn’t prepared for just how intense it would be.

At the next table, several young locals laughed while complaining about work and dipping food into bowls of garlic oil sauce. They were speaking in Chongqing dialect, which is similar to Chengdu dialect but has a more distinct, lively tone. I couldn’t understand every word, but I could tell they were having a good time—joking, laughing, and enjoying each other’s company. It was a reminder that, for all its futuristic visuals, Chongqing is a city of people.

I tried a piece of tripe. It was tender, chewy, and full of the spicy soup flavor. By the third bite, I was sweating. My nose was running, my eyes were watering, but I couldn’t stop eating. There’s something addictive about Chongqing hotpot—it’s painful, but in the best way possible. It’s a sensory overload, just like the city itself.

But strangely happy. Because Chongqing hotpot is not only food. It is emotional release. The city is chaotic, fast-paced, and overwhelming—and hotpot is a way to slow down, let go, and just be in the moment. In a city where everything feels like it’s moving too fast, hotpot is a chance to pause and connect with others.

The city does not ask:

  • Who are you?

  • Where are you from?

  • What do you do?

It simply gives you:

  • a boiling pot,

  • a stool,

  • strong chili oil,

  • and permission to disappear into the moment.

By the end of that meal, I felt like Chongqing had accepted me. The waiter, a friendly local woman, noticed I was sweating and brought me a bowl of cold soybean milk—complimentary, she said, “for the spicy.” It was a small gesture, but it made me feel welcome. As a Chinese traveler, I’ve found that locals in Chongqing are some of the most generous and welcoming people in China—they take pride in their city, and they love sharing it with others.

Why Foreign Travelers Are Obsessed With Chongqing

In recent years, Chongqing has exploded across TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, and Reddit. As a Chinese blogger who follows international travel trends, I’ve watched this happen firsthand. Foreign travelers are obsessed with Chongqing—and it’s easy to see why. It’s a side of China they never expected to see: not ancient temples, not traditional gardens, but a giant futuristic megacity hidden deep inside the mountains. For many foreigners, China is still associated with pandas, the Great Wall, and traditional culture—but Chongqing shatters that stereotype, showing a modern, futuristic side of China that few people knew existed.

For many international travelers, it represents a side of China they never expected to see. Not ancient temples. Not traditional gardens. But a giant futuristic megacity hidden deep inside the mountains. I’ve met dozens of foreign travelers in Chongqing—from the US, Europe, Australia—and they all say the same thing: “I had no idea China had a city like this.” It’s a revelation for them, and it’s changing the way the world sees China.

And Chongqing is actively embracing that image. The local government has invested heavily in tourism, promoting Chongqing’s cyberpunk aesthetic and making the city more international-friendly. They’ve added English signs, improved public transportation, and even launched “cyberpunk night tours” that take visitors to the most visually striking spots in the city.

Drone light shows now illuminate the skyline. Night tourism routes attract millions. Observation decks overflow with photographers. Foreign influencers describe it as:

  • “the closest city to Blade Runner”

  • “China’s cyberpunk capital”

  • “the most futuristic city on Earth”

I’ve seen these videos on YouTube and TikTok, and the comments are full of people saying they want to visit Chongqing. It’s amazing to see a Chinese city get this kind of international attention—and it’s well-deserved. Chongqing is unique, and it’s a city that needs to be seen to be believed.

At the 2026 Chongqing International Travel Agents Conference, nearly 300 guests from around 40 countries gathered in the city. That would have sounded unimaginable decades ago, when Chongqing was still associated with isolation and difficult mountain travel. When I was a child, my parents told me that Chongqing was a “hard place to reach”—a city hidden in the mountains, with poor transportation and few attractions. How things have changed.

Now, it has become one of China’s strongest international urban brands. It’s not just a tourist destination; it’s a symbol of China’s modernization, a city that has transformed itself from a remote mountain town to a global megacity. As a Chinese blogger, I’m proud of Chongqing—not just for its beauty, but for its resilience and innovation.

More Than a Viral City

Many internet-famous cities attract attention briefly and disappear. In China, we’ve seen this happen with small towns and villages that go viral for a single attraction—only to be forgotten a few months later. But Chongqing feels different. It’s not just a “viral city”; it’s a real, thriving metropolis with a rich culture and a strong identity.

Because behind the “cyberpunk” visuals is a complete urban system:

  • advanced transportation, including high-speed trains, metro lines, and bridges that connect every part of the city;

  • massive infrastructure that can handle millions of visitors every year;

  • tourism optimization, with English signs, international-friendly hotels, and tour guides who speak multiple languages;

  • mobile payment convenience—like most Chinese cities, Chongqing is cashless, with WeChat Pay and Alipay accepted everywhere, making it easy for foreign visitors to get around;

  • international-friendly services, including airports with direct flights to major cities around the world;

  • and a deeply unique local culture, from hotpot to dialect to traditional festivals.

The city has successfully transformed:

  • geographic disadvantages

  • into

  • unforgettable identity.

That is the real reason Chongqing works. Its magic is not fake. It’s not a constructed tourist trap; it’s a natural result of the city’s geography, history, and people. It grew naturally from mountains, rivers, engineering, chaos, and human adaptation. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve visited many tourist cities in China, and most feel “manufactured”—but Chongqing feels real. It’s messy, it’s chaotic, and it’s authentic.

Chongqing Is Not Escapism — It Is Controlled Chaos

What surprised me most was not the skyline. It was how the city changed my mental state. As someone who lives a busy life in Chengdu—running a blog, meeting deadlines, and constantly planning my next trip—I’m used to being in control. I make to-do lists, I plan my days, and I hate being lost or disoriented. But in Chongqing, that all changed.

In Chongqing, planning becomes meaningless. The city is too chaotic, too unpredictable. You can’t plan your route because GPS doesn’t work. You can’t plan your day because you might get lost and stumble upon something amazing. You stop trying to control everything.

You simply walk. Climb. Get lost. Sweat. Eat. Look around. I spent my days in Chongqing wandering aimlessly—no plan, no destination. I’d walk up a staircase and find a hidden park. I’d turn a corner and find a street food market. I’d get lost and end up in a neighborhood where locals waved and said hello. It was liberating.

And somewhere between a staircase, a monorail, and a bowl of noodles in a tiny alley, your anxiety becomes smaller. The city’s chaos is overwhelming, but it’s a good kind of overwhelming. It distracts you from your worries, makes you focus on the present moment, and reminds you that life doesn’t have to be perfect.

Chongqing does not heal you gently. It overwhelms you until your inner noise disappears. It’s not a city that coddles you; it’s a city that challenges you, pushes you out of your comfort zone, and makes you feel alive. I left Chongqing feeling lighter, more relaxed, and more appreciative of the chaos in life.

Maybe that is why so many people become emotionally attached to this city. Because here, life does not need to be perfectly organized. It only needs to feel alive. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve never felt this way about a city before. Chongqing doesn’t just entertain you; it changes you. It makes you see life differently—less about control, more about embracing the chaos.

Final Thoughts

If someone asked me which city best represents the futuristic feeling of modern China, my answer would be simple:

Chongqing.

Not because it looks futuristic. There are other Chinese cities with more modern skyscrapers, more advanced technology, and more sleek infrastructure. But none of them have the soul, the chaos, the humanity of Chongqing.

But because it turned impossible geography into an entirely new kind of urban civilization. A civilization where nature and technology coexist, where chaos and order balance each other, and where ordinary people live in a setting that feels like a sci-fi movie.

A place where:

  • trains pass through buildings,

  • bridges float between mountains,

  • neon lights reflect on river fog,

  • and ordinary life feels cinematic.

Chongqing is not science fiction. It is what happens when reality becomes stranger, louder, hotter, and more layered than imagination itself. As a Chinese blogger, I’ve traveled the world, but no city has left an impression on me quite like Chongqing. It’s a city that defies expectations, challenges norms, and reminds us that the most amazing things in life are often the ones we never saw coming.

For anyone who loves cyberpunk, travel, or just wants to experience something truly unique, Chongqing is a must-visit. It’s not just a city—it’s an experience, one that will stay with you long after you leave. And as a Chinese local, I’m proud to call it one of the most amazing cities in my country.
